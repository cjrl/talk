A primer on mod 2

Mod 2 arithmetic, written $\mathbb F_2$, has just two numbers, 0 and 1, with one rule: $1+1=0$.

In logic terms: addition is XOR and multiplication is AND.

We can do linear algebra over $\mathbb F_2$: vectors and matrices are built from 0s and 1s, and we add and multiply them with these two tables.

In this talk we will work mod 2. Homology will be mod 2 homology.

Homology = cycles modulo boundaries

Chain

A $k$-chain is a formal sum of $k$-dimensional pieces: points, edges, faces for $k=0,1,2$.

Mod 2: each piece is in the sum or not. These sums form $C_k$.

Cycle

A cycle is a chain with no loose ends: $\partial c=0$.

A path has two endpoints, so it is not a cycle.

Boundary

A boundary is the edge of something one dimension higher: $c=\partial d$.

Every boundary is a cycle: the edge of a disk has no edge.

Homology

Cycles that are not boundaries, counting two as the same if they differ by a boundary.

That counts the holes.

$$\cdots\longrightarrow C_{k+1}\xrightarrow{\ \partial_{k+1}\ }C_k\xrightarrow{\ \partial_k\ }C_{k-1}\longrightarrow\cdots\qquad\partial_k\circ\partial_{k+1}=0$$
$$H_k\;=\;\frac{\color{#0f7f86}\ker\partial_k}{\color{#b4690e}\operatorname{im}\partial_{k+1}}\qquad\text{(cycles modulo boundaries)}$$

Groups

What is a group?

A group is a set $G$ with a way to multiply two elements so that

  • multiplication is associative,
  • there is an identity $1$,
  • every element has an inverse.

Example. $C_4=\{1,g,g^2,g^3\}$ with $g^4=1$. Here $g$ is a quarter turn of a square.

“Groups are meant to act on things.”
Group actions

A group acting on a space

$G$ acts on a space $X$ when each $g\in G$ moves the points of $X$: $x\mapsto g\cdot x$.

Two rules:

  • doing nothing does nothing: $1\cdot x=x$,
  • moving twice is moving by the product: $(gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)$.

Example. $C_4$ acts on a circle by quarter turns. The orbit of a point is where it goes: $x,\ gx,\ g^2x,\ g^3x$.

Smith theory

Homology controls fixed points

If a 2-group $G$ acts on a nice space $X$, the fixed set $X^G$ cannot be arbitrary. Its mod 2 homology is controlled by the mod 2 homology of $X$.

Acyclic goes to acyclic

$\tilde H_*(X)=0\ \Rightarrow\ \tilde H_*(X^G)=0$

Smith, 1938 to 1941. If $X$ has the homology of a point, so does $X^G$. Next slide.

Size goes down

$\dim H_*(X^G)\le\dim H_*(X)$

Floyd, 1952. The fixed set has at most as much total homology.

Smith's theorem for acyclic spaces

Smith, 1938 to 1941
A finite 2-group $G$ acts on a sufficiently nice space $X$. Then
$$\tilde H_*(X)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\tilde H_*(X^G)=0.$$
In particular $X^G\ne\emptyset$.

Example. $G=C_4$ acting on the disk $D^2$

$\tilde H_*(D^2)=0$, so $X$ is acyclic.

Why a chromatic setting?

Algebraic topology always wants better, more refined invariants.

At the prime 2, the Morava K-theories $K(n)$ interpolate between rational homology and mod 2 homology.

$K(0)$
rational homology
$K(1)$
related to K-theory of vector bundles
$K(2),K(3),\dots$
everything in between
$K(\infty)$
ordinary mod 2 homology

Each height sees at least as much as the one before: $k_0(X)\le k_1(X)\le\cdots\le k_\infty(X)$.

Think of a prism splitting light into colours: each level shows a different layer of the same space. Hence chromatic.

The chromatic setting

Smith theory lives at the top height, $n=\infty$. We can ask the same question at every height $K(n)$. Write $k_n(X)=\dim K(n)_*(X)$.

Symmetry moves us between heights: the fixed points are seen at a lower height than the space. How far down?

Chromatic Smith $(G,n,m)$
$\tilde K(m)_*(X)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \tilde K(n)_*(X^G)=0$
Chromatic Floyd $(G,n,m)$
$k_m(X)\ \ge\ k_n(X^G)$

For every sufficiently nice $G$-space $X$, with $G$ a finite 2-group. The height can only drop going to the fixed points: from $m$ down to $n$.

Classical (mod 2 homology)
Chromatic (Morava K-theory)
Floyd $\Rightarrow$ Smith
Known. A short argument.
Immediate from the definitions.
Smith $\Rightarrow$ Floyd
Now a consequence of the chromatic theorem.
Theorem, Kuhn–Lloyd Chromatic Smith implies chromatic Floyd. The two are equivalent.

Idea. If Floyd fails, take a space $X$ that violates it. Cut a summand out of $X^{\wedge k}$ using an idempotent in $\mathbb Z_{(2)}[\Sigma_k]$. That summand violates Smith. (An idea of J. Smith, used by Hopkins and Smith.)

The blue shift number

Blue shift number
$r_n(G)$ is the smallest $r$ such that for every sufficiently nice $G$-space $X$,
$$\tilde K(n+r)_*(X)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \tilde K(n)_*(X^G)=0.$$
It measures the drop in height from $X$ to its fixed points $X^G$.

Part of the game: determine the minimum drop in height.

Conjecture (Kuhn–Lloyd)
$r_n(G)=r_+(G)$ for every finite 2-group $G$ and height $n$. Here $r_+(G)$ is the length of the shortest chain $1=K_0\trianglelefteq K_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq K_r=G$ with every quotient $K_i/K_{i-1}$ cyclic: the cyclic length of $G$. In particular, the drop does not depend on $n$.
Always $0\le r_n(G)\le\log_2|G|$.
$C_2$ acting: $r=1$ (Strickland; Balmer–Sanders).
Abelian $G$: $r=\operatorname{rank}(G)$ (Barthel et al.).
Extraspecial 2-groups: determined by Kuhn–Lloyd.
Resolved
OpenAI has posted a preprint proving it: $r_n(G)=r_+(G)$ for every finite 2-group and every height.
Cyclic length and chromatic fixed-point loss, OpenAI Preprint #314, September 2026.
My research

Using the general theory

Now that we have this general theory, we can use it to calculate the Morava K-theory of spaces.

1. Blue shift lower bound

If a cyclic 2-group $C$ acts on $X$, the blue shift bound gives $k_n(X)\ge k_{n-1}(X^C)$.

2. Spectral sequence upper bound

The Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence gives $k_n(X)\le\dim H(X;Q_n)$, the Margolis homology of the $n$th Milnor operation $Q_n$.

3. They meet

Then $k_n(X)$ is known exactly, and the spectral sequence collapses.

I searched for group actions on real Grassmannians whose fixed points make the bounds meet.

This was my dissertation. It computes $k_n(\mathrm{Gr}_d(\mathbb R^m))=\binom md$ for $m\le 2^{n+1}$, and $k_n(\mathrm{Gr}_2(\mathbb R^m))$ for all $m$.

This involved writing Python and GAP code to search for these actions using representation theory.

The problem: airborne holding

Air traffic control may hold an aircraft in a racetrack pattern over a fix while it waits for clearance.

The pattern: a straight leg to the fix, a turn, a leg back, a turn, and again.

Problem: from the radar track alone, decide when an aircraft was holding.

Topology in Aviation

How would we detect it?

A radar track is a list of timestamped positions.

We want a test that is quick and survives noise.

Let's model it topologically.

Treat the track as a shape, and ask what shape it is.

Topology in Aviation

Modeling the track topologically

Motivation: transversality (Thom, 1954)
A generic curve crosses itself only transversely (at a genuine angle, not touching along a tangent), and transverse crossings survive small pushoffs. Holding lies on top of itself, so it is not generic: its pushoff meetings come and go.

1. Smooth the track into a linestring.

2. Treat it as a curve in the plane. Is it an embedding? Airborne holding crosses itself.

3. Copy the curve and push it off by a constant distance. Count where the copy meets the original. Is the count stable as the distance changes?

R. Thom, Quelques propriétés globales des variétés différentiables, Comment. Math. Helv. 28 (1954), 17–86. See also Guillemin–Pollack, Differential Topology, Ch. 2.

The Euler characteristic

A signed count of the cells of a space:

$$\chi(X)=\#\text{vertices}-\#\text{edges}+\#\text{faces}-\cdots$$
$V$$E$$F$$V-E+F$
Tetrahedron4642
Cube81262
Torus, $6\times 4$ grid2448240

Euler, 1750: every convex polyhedron has $V-E+F=2$.

It only depends on the space

Cut the space into cells in any way you like and you get the same number. It is built from homology (Poincaré, 1895):
$\chi(X)=\sum_k(-1)^k\dim H_k(X)$.

Two examples

Sphere: $1-0+1=2$. Torus: $1-2+1=0$.

Why it matters here. It is a count of cells, so it behaves well when a group moves the cells around: a free orbit has size divisible by 2, so it disappears mod 2. That is the next slide.

It also proves Smith's theorem for acyclic spaces: $\chi(X)=1$ forces $\chi(X^G)\equiv 1\pmod 2$, so $X^G\neq\emptyset$. See "Floyd implies Smith".

Backup · Smith theory

Floyd implies Smith

Floyd
(1) $\dim H_*(X^G)\le\dim H_*(X)$
(2) $\chi(X^G)\equiv\chi(X)\pmod 2$
Smith
$X$ acyclic $\Rightarrow$ $X^G$ acyclic

Let $X$ be acyclic: $H_*(X)=\mathbb F_2$, so $\chi(X)=1$.

Size. By (1), $\dim H_*(X^G)\le 1$.

Euler characteristic. By (2), $\chi(X^G)\equiv 1\pmod 2$, so $\chi(X^G)\ne 0$. Hence $X^G\ne\emptyset$ and $H_0(X^G)\ne 0$.

Conclusion. $H_*(X^G)=\mathbb F_2$, concentrated in degree 0. So $X^G$ is acyclic. $\blacksquare$

Floyd is the stronger theorem: it works for every space, and it gives Smith for free.

Real Grassmannians

$\mathrm{Gr}_k(\mathbb R^n)$ is the space of all $k$-dimensional subspaces of $\mathbb R^n$.

SpaceDimensionCells
$\mathrm{Gr}_1(\mathbb R^{n})=\mathbb{RP}^{n-1}$$n-1$$n$
$\mathrm{Gr}_2(\mathbb R^3)\cong\mathbb{RP}^2$23
$\mathrm{Gr}_2(\mathbb R^4)$46

$\mathrm{Gr}_k(\mathbb R^n)$$k(n-k)$$\binom nk$

A plane in $\mathbb R^3$ is determined by the line perpendicular to it.

Cells

Row reduce a $k\times n$ matrix whose rows span the subspace. The positions of the leading 1s name a Schubert cell, and the free entries are its coordinates.

Homology

Mod 2, each cell contributes one class, so $\dim H_*(\mathrm{Gr}_k(\mathbb R^n))=\binom nk$.

Why it matters here. A group acting through a representation of $G$ on $\mathbb R^n$ acts on every $\mathrm{Gr}_k(\mathbb R^n)$. The fixed points split into products of smaller Grassmannians.
